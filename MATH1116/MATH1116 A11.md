# 2:
We aim to demonstrate that $g$ is differentiable first 
IE, $\forall x, y\in \mathbb{R}^n,\ g(x)=g(y)+T_{g}(y-x)+\lVert y-x \rVert\epsilon_{g}(y-x)$
$\implies g(x)-g(y)=T(y-x)+\lVert y-x \rVert\epsilon y-x$


Let $x,y \in \mathbb{R}^n$
Define $p: \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R}  \quad (t,x)\mapsto (x_{1}t+x_{2}t^2+\dots+x_{n}t^n)$
fix $t \in \mathbb{R}$
$\implies g(x)-g(y)=\int_{0}^1f(t,p(t,x))dt- \int_{0}^1f(t,p(t,y))dt$
$= \int_{0}^1 f(0,(x_{1}-y_{1})t+\dots+(x_{n}-y_{n})t^n)dt$
Call $v_{t}=(0,p(t,(x-y)))$
$\implies g(x)-g(y)=\int_{0}^1[T_{f}(v_{t})+ ||v_{t}||\epsilon_{f}(v_{t})]dt$
Define $T_{g}(x):=\int_{0}^1T_{f}(0,p(t,x))$ for any $t \in \mathbb{R}$

Because $T_{f}$ is linear by differentiability of $f$, and the definite integral is linear, and composition of linear maps is linear, then $T_{g}(x)$ is linear. 

Now we aim to show that $\exists \epsilon:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ : $\epsilon(x)\xrightarrow[x \to 0]{}0$
Define 
$w= (y_{1}-x_{1},\dots,y_{n}-x_{n})$
$||w||\epsilon_{g}(w):=\int_{0}^1 \lVert v \rVert \epsilon_{f}(v)dt$
We aim to show $e_{g}(w)\xrightarrow[w \to 0]{}0$
$\epsilon_{g}(w)=\frac{1}{\lVert w \rVert}\int_{0}^1 \lVert v \rVert\epsilon_{f}(v)dt$

Note that $\lVert v \rVert= \lvert p(t,v) \rvert=\lvert  \langle \begin{bmatrix}t  \\  t^2   \\  . \\  . \\ t^n \end{bmatrix},w \rangle \rvert \leq \lVert w \rVert \cdot C$
$\implies \epsilon_{g}(w)\leq \frac{1}{\lVert w \rVert} \int_{0}^1 C \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert \epsilon_{f}(v)dt$

Note $\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert$ doesnt depend on $t$

Additionally $v= (0, \langle \begin{bmatrix}t  \\  t^2   \\  . \\  . \\ t^n \end{bmatrix},w \rangle)$ so $\lim_{ w \to 0 }v=(0,0)$ (inner product axioms)
Thus
$\epsilon_{g}(w)\leq C\int_{0}^1 \epsilon_{f}(v)dt \xrightarrow[w \to 0]{}0$ 
So $g$ is differentiable $\blacksquare$.

$\impliedby$
Let $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$, and $V$ be a finite dimensional inner product space. 
Suppose there was some $U \subseteq V$ such that $T=P_{U}$ 
$\implies \forall \mathbf{v}\in V,\quad \exists \mathbf{u}\in U\ \ \exists \mathbf{w}\in U^\perp: \mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u+w}$ 

So for $\mathbf{v_{1}},\mathbf{v_{2}}\in V$
$\langle \mathbf{v_{1}},\mathbf{v_{2}} \rangle=\langle \mathbf{u_{1}}+\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}+\mathbf{w}_{2} \rangle$
So, $\langle T\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2} \rangle=\langle \mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u_{2}}+\mathbf{w}_{2} \rangle$
$=\langle \mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2} \rangle+\langle \mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2} \rangle$
$= \langle \mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2} \rangle$  (via definition of orthogonal complement)
And because $T=P_{U}$, $T(\mathbf{v}_{2})=\mathbf{u}_{2}$
So $\langle T\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2} \rangle=\langle \mathbf{v}_{1},T\mathbf{v}_{2} \rangle$ so $T$ is self adjoint.


$\implies$
Let $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$, be self adjoint. 
Notice that $V=\ker(T) \oplus range(T)$
$\implies V=\ker(T^*) \oplus range(T)$     ($T$ is self adjoint)
Note as well that $\ker(T^*) =range(T)^\perp$
Let $\mathbf{v}\in V$
$\implies \exists \mathbf{u} \in \ker(T^*)^\perp,\ \ \exists \mathbf{w}\in range(T)$ such that $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}$
$\implies T(\mathbf{v})=T(\mathbf{u})+T(\mathbf{w})$
$\implies T(\mathbf{v})=T(\mathbf{w})$
Because $\mathbf{w}\in Range(T),\exists \mathbf{x}\in V$ such that $T(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{w}$
$\implies T(\mathbf{v})=T(T(\mathbf{x}))$
But because $T^2=T$
$T\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{w}$ 
Thus, $T=P_{U}$, where $U=range(T)$
