$\left[ P_{u} \right]_\mathcal{E}$ is the representation of $P_{U}$ in terms of $\mathcal{E}$ vectors. 

Observe 
$P_{U}(\mathbf{e}_{1})=\sum \langle \mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{i} \rangle\mathbf{u_{i}}$ (via warmup 1(f))
So the first column of $[P_{U}]_{\mathcal{E}}$ is $\left[ \sum \langle \mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{i} \rangle\mathbf{u}_{i} \right]_{\mathcal{E}} = \sum \langle \mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{i} \rangle \left[ \mathbf{u}_{i} \right]_{\mathcal{E}}$
Note that this corresponds to matrix vector products. 


Note that additionally, the $i$th column of $A$ is $[\mathbf{u}_{i}]_{\mathcal{E}}$. 
So, the first column of $\left[ P_{U} \right]_{\mathcal{E}}= \sum \langle \mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{i} \rangle[\mathbf{u}_{i}]=A \times \begin{bmatrix} \langle \mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{1} \rangle  \\  . \\  . \\  . \\  \langle \mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{k} \rangle\end{bmatrix}$ via the definition of matrix vector product.


The second column is $A \times \begin{bmatrix}\langle \mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{u_{2}} \rangle \\  . \\  . \\  . \\  \langle \mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{u}_{k} \rangle\end{bmatrix}$


Thus, the product of these columns (the inner products in vectors) together in a matrix, and $A$ will give $\left[ P_{U} \right]_{\mathcal{E}}$, via the definition of matrix multiplication.






So, if we can prove that $A^{\dagger}=\begin{bmatrix}\langle \mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{1} \rangle & . & . & . & \langle \mathbf{e}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{1} \rangle \\   & .\\   . &  & . &  & .\\ .  &  &  & . \\ . &  &  &  & . \\  . &  &  &  & . \\  \\   \langle \mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{k} \rangle & . & . & . & \langle \mathbf{e}_{n},\mathbf{u}_{k} \rangle\end{bmatrix}$  

Then we have shown that the equality holds. So, we want to show that $A^{\dagger}_{j,i}= \langle \mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{j} \rangle$



We do this by deriving the values of $A$.

The $i$th column of $A$ is $[\mathbf{u}_{i}]_{\mathcal{E}}$ 
So take $u_{i}=a_{1,i}\mathbf{e_{1}}+\dots+a_{n,i}\mathbf{e}_{n}$ 

Then, to extract $a_{j,i}$ (the $j$th entry of the $i$th column), we can do this via the inner product 
$\langle \mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{e}_{j} \rangle= a_{1,j}\langle  \mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{j}\rangle+\dots+a_{n,j}\langle \mathbf{e}_{n},\mathbf{e}_{j} \rangle$

$\implies \langle \mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{e}_{j} \rangle=a_{j,i} \langle \mathbf{e}_{j},\mathbf{e}_{j} \rangle$ ($\mathcal{E}$ is orthogonal so all other elements $=0$)

So, our matrix A has $A_{j,i}=\langle \mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{e}_j \rangle$

Now, $A^{\dagger}_{j,i}=\bar{A}^T_{j,i}=\overline{A_{i,j}}=\overline{\langle \mathbf{u}_{j}, \mathbf{e}_{i}\rangle}=$ $\langle \mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{j} \rangle$ via conjugate symmetry 

Thus, $A^{\dagger}$ matches the matrix above, so therefore $AA^{\dagger}=[P_{U}]_{\mathcal{E}}$

$A\left( \frac{1}{2}(T+T^*)  \right)=\frac{1}{2}A(T)+\frac{1}{2}A(T^*)\\=\frac{1}{2}T^* +\frac{1}{2}T^{**}\\=\frac{1}{2}(T+T^*)$ so $\lambda=1$ 

$A\left( \frac{1}{2}(T-T^*) \right)=\frac{1}{2}(A(T))-\frac{1}{2}(A(T^*))\\=\frac{1}{2}T^* +\frac{1}{2}T{**}=-1 \cdot\left( \frac{1}{2}(T-T^*) \right)$ Thus has an eigenvalue of $-1$

b:
Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue for $A$

$\implies \exists T \in \mathcal{L}(V) \text{ non zero}:\ \ A(T)=\lambda T$
$\implies T^*=\lambda T$
$\implies A(T^*)=A(\lambda T)$
$\implies T=\lambda(A(T))$
$\implies T=\lambda^2T$ (T is an eigenvector for $\mathcal{A}$)
$\implies T(1-\lambda^2)=0$
$\implies \lambda=\pm {1}$ because $T$ is non zero.
Thus our eigenvalues must be $1,-1$

c:
Let $q(z)=z^2-1$
We aim to show that $q(A)$ is the zero operator 
let $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$
$\implies q(A) (T) =A^2(T)-(T)=T^{**}-T=\mathbf{0}$
Now we show that $q(z)$ is minimal. 
Suppose that some polynomial of degree one existed, and $c_{1},c_{2} \in \mathbb{R}\setminus \{ \mathbf{0} \}$
$\implies q(z)=c_{1}z+c_{2} =\mathbf{0}\forall z \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}(V))$, 
In particular, choose $\mathcal{A}$, and $Id_{V}\in \mathcal{L}(V)$

$\implies c_{1}I+c_{2}I=0$
