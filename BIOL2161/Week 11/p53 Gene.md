p53 [[Gene]]
p53 is the protein assigned to the TP53 gene, but is colloquially known as just the p53 gene. 

The p53 gene is a master regulator of genome policing mechanisms. It does not directly repair [[DNA]], neither induce apoptosis. 

p53 decides which of the downstream programs should be activated, which is where it gets its nickname as the "guardian of the genome"


## Assisted control of cell division 
When a cell first starts demonstrating cancerous properties due to [[DNA]] damage or oncogenic [[mutation]]s, the first response is to **halt** the cell cycle and isolate the cell from further proliferations 

If damage in the DNA can be repaired, the cell may return to normal function. However, if the damage is too severe or persistent, the cell either undergoes senescence (permanently arrested) or undergoes apoptosis.

## Effects of missing [[p53 Gene]]
One good example is in mice with germline deleterious mutations to one copy of p53. These mice are very [[Cancer]] prone and often acquire cancer very early. 
Mice missing both copies almost always die prenatally

Over half of all cancers have a mutation in the p53 gene, and most of the otehrs have a mutation somewhere in the p53 signalling pathway. 

## Gene dosage 
Different levels of p53 accumulation trigger different cellular outcomes, ranging from DNA repair and cell cycle arrest to apoptosis 

The quantity of it is regulated by synthesis, degradation and phosphorylation 


# As a TF 
p53 is a [[Transcription factor]] that is activated by cellular stress, especially [[DNA]] damage 

Under normal conditions, p53 is constantly produced degraded. p53 is negatively regulated by MDM2 
![[MDM2]]


## In healthy cells 
In healthy cells, p53 is repressed. The mechanism for this is that p53 and MDM2 are created in a complex in the nucleus and the when shuttled into the cytoplasm, **proteasomes** uses the ubiquitination signal to degrade p53.

the MDM2 then travels back into the cytoplasm to ubiquitinate more p53 
Note that ubiquitination occurs before shuttling into cytoplasm 

If this were not to occur, accumulation of p53 would eventually lead to apoptosis 


# In stressed cells 
When DNA is damaged, ATM or ATR kinases are activated. 
ATM and ATR respond to slightly different signals -
ATM primarily responds to [[Double stranded DNA break]], while ATR responds to replication stress and single stranded DNA exposure caused by things such as stalled replication forks and UV damage. 

ATM/R start phosphorylation on both MDM2 and p53. This produces a conformational change, detaching it from MDM2. Because of this, it never gets shuttled into cytoplasm and accumulates in the nucleus rapidly. 

p53 then creates a tetramer (four subunits) and from there binds to the DNA. It acts as a TF and enhancers tumour suppressant genes. 


## Stress signals that p53 can respond to

DNA damage is a good example of an intrinsic signal, but there are many extrinsic signals, such as response to TNF$\alpha$ (tumour necrosis factor alpha)
TNF$\alpha$ is a secreted extracellular signalling protein that is secreted by activated immune cells 
This is an organism response to cancer from immune cells. They detect the cancer and tell the cells around to die.  TNF response is a good example of a method surrounding how a cell can respond to external stress signals, even when by itself it hasnt detected any internal damage. 

## TNF pathway  
TNF alpha binds to the receptor and sends a signal to the p38 kinase. It phosphorylates p53 and the same action of binding to DNA is done. 


## Specialised forms of p53
[[Alternative splicing]] is the method that eukaryotes use to create different variants of p53 

This is necessary for a few reasons: 
The role of isoforms created through [[Alternative splicing]] is to modulate the character of the response. When things go bad, immediate response is needed. This is why base p53 is always present and degraded continuously by mdm2. Once response has started and cell cycle has been halted, the cell can tailor its p53 forms more carefully.
