Regulation

Every cell in the body contains essentially the same genome.
Biological complexity does not come from size of genome, but rather the nature of said [[Gene regulation]]
**one [[Gene]] does not equal one protein**

Levels of gene regulation in [[Eukaryotic cells|eukaryote]]s
- [[Chromatin]] level (methylation, structure)
- transcriptional level ([[activator protein]], repressors, etc)
- [[RNA splicing|RNA processing]]
- Coordinated gene expression - when more than one gene is necessary for a protein 
- RNA interference, between different RNA molecules miRNA, siRNA 
- post translational regulation 
- Crispr 


![[Chromatin]]

The state of chromatin determines whether a gene is available for [[Transcription]] - must be euchromatin 

A gene located in heterochromatin is effectively off. 

for a gene to be transcribed, the promoter and key regulatory elements must be accessible, and the gene body must be in a chromatin state that allows for [[RNA polymerase II]] to pass. 

Starting transcription requires a fully accessible promoter, while elongation can occur through chromatin that is actively being remodelled. [[RNA polymerase II]] can unwind nucleosomes itself. 

Chromatin can be remodelled via several mechanisms 
Histone modifications:
- Histone acetylation - opens chromatin up and promotes [[Transcription]]
- Histone methylation - acivates or represses transcription - is context dependent. 
DNA methylation: Modifications to DNA directly. It acts as a signal that recruits proteins which compact chromatin and silence gene expression. 

All chromatin remodelling is done via proteins with regulatory signals. These proteins are called **transcription factors**

Transcription factors
These modify chromatin at specific genes via response to cellular signals. They directly bind to DNA. Any other regulatory proteins act indirectly via modification of chromatin, or interacting with [[Transcription]] factors. 

All transcription factors are proteins. they regulate gene expression by binding to specific DNA sequences, acting as switches to turn genes on or off.

Topologically associating domain 
aliases: TAD 
Chromatin is organised into regulatory regions, each about 100kb-1mb long. Chromatin state of each TAD can be regulated independently. 
These were discovered in 2012. 
Tads are 3d genomic domains that contain multiple chromatin loops. These loops bring distant genomic elements into close proximity. 
![[Pasted image 20260430162559.png]]

TADs contain genes and their regulatory elements. 
TADs are typically defined by boundary elements. These are the "insulators"
Most transcriptional regulation occurs within a TAD, but sometimes it occurs between TADs. These exceptions are typically less frequent and are usually more constrained.
