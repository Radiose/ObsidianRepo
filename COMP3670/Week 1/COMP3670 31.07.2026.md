# Mathematical notation
$\mathcal{D}=\{ (\mathbf{x_{1}},y_{2}),\dots, (\mathbf{x_{n}},y_{n})\}$ with $\mathbf{x_{i}}\in \mathcal{X}$ and $y_{i}\in \mathcal{Y}$
Hypothesis set: $\mathcal{H}$ (set of candidate functions $h:\mathcal{X}\to \mathcal{Y}$)
In sample error: $E_{in}(h)$ - the error of $h$ on $\mathcal{D}$
$E_{in}(h)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{1}(h(\mathbf{x_{i}})\not=y_{i})$
in which $\mathbb{1}\{ \cdot \}=1$ if the expression inside is true, and $\mathbb{1}\{ \cdot \}=0$ if false. 
Learning from data:

$\hat{h}=\text{arg min}_{h \in \mathcal{H}}E_{in}(h)$-find the rule that minimises the error.

Generalisation and probability 
$E_{in}(h)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{1}(h(\mathbf{x_{i}})\not=y_{i})$
We can compute this exactly for the training data.
The [[The perceptron|PLA]] drives $E_{in}(\hat{h})$ to 0.
We cannot guarantee that $E_{in}(\hat{h})\approx E_{out}(\hat{h})$ on data outside of the sample, but we can ask how probable it is that $E_{in}(\hat{h})$ and $E_{out}(\hat{h})$ are far apart.
Statistics will let us
	quantify the gap between $E_{in}(\hat{h})$ and $E_{out}(\hat{h})$
	determine how much data is needed for reliable generalisation 
	understand how the complexity of $\mathcal{H}$ determines the gap
